What Is Discriminated unions?

Discriminated unions, in one sentence: types with a fixed set of named cases. In practice that means the details are worth getting right once, because everything downstream inherits the decision. Small teams benefit as much as large ones, often faster because they can change habits quickly.

The mental model is simple: one clear definition, one clear workflow, and configuration that matches reality. Most confusion around discriminated unions comes from mixing those layers — mixing definitions from different eras, or configs copied from projects with different needs. Keep them separate and the rest of this guide follows naturally.

Common Discriminated unions Mistakes

The same failure patterns show up again and again. Here are the four we meet most often, including the classic: Duplicating DU cases across files and drifting out of sync.

  • Letting local settings drift from CI until nobody knows which one is right.
  • Optimizing before measuring, then optimizing again after the metrics disagree.
  • Assuming the default configuration matches your project's real requirements.
  • Shipping changes without a rollback plan and improvising during the incident.

Every one of these fixes is cheap when caught early and expensive when caught late. A quarterly review of discriminated unions — twenty minutes, honestly — catches most of them before they metastasize.

Performance Considerations

Change one thing at a time. Stacked optimizations hide which change actually helped and which added complexity for nothing. Re-run the same measurement after each adjustment to discriminated unions and keep a short log of results. When two changes interact, test them separately before combining, or you will ship mystery performance.

Measure first. Capture baseline numbers for the scenario you care about before touching anything, because intuition about hot paths is famously wrong. For discriminated unions, that usually means timing the end-to-end operation, not the method you suspect, and recording memory alongside time. A ten-minute baseline turns a week of speculation into an afternoon of targeted work.

Discriminated unions vs Fable for frontend

They overlap enough that the question is usually about trade-offs rather than right and wrong. Discriminated unions optimizes for invalid states become unrepresentable; exhaustiveness is compiler-checked; Fable for frontend pushes harder on the opposite axis. List your constraints honestly — team experience, platform targets, and maintenance budget — and the answer usually picks itself.

Start from the problem, not the tool. If your pain is invalid states become unrepresentable; exhaustiveness is compiler-checked, Discriminated unions is the more direct route; if your team already lives in the other ecosystem, Fable for frontend minimizes ramp-up cost. Version Notes for 2026

Compatibility rarely breaks loudly. Small behavior changes in discriminated unions accumulate until something subtle fails in production weeks later. Keep a changelog of your own: which versions you adopted, when, and what you had to adjust. That log becomes the fastest diagnostic when old behavior needs explaining.

A Practical Workflow

A repeatable workflow turns discriminated unions from a personality trait into a team capability:

  1. Pick the smallest meaningful change you can ship and measure — momentum matters more than scope.
  2. Write the chosen setup down in the repository so the decision survives the next refactor.
  3. Automate the step so it runs without anyone remembering the manual command.
  4. Schedule a short review each quarter to prune what no longer earns its place.
  5. Document the rollback path before you need it, not during an incident.

This loop — audit, change, automate, verify — is deliberately generic. It fits F# at any scale and survives every tooling generation we have seen.
