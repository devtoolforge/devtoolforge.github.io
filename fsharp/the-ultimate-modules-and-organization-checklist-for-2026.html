What Is Modules and organization?

Modules and organization, in one sentence: namespaces plus modules for grouping functions. In practice that means the details are worth getting right once, because everything downstream inherits the decision. Small teams benefit as much as large ones, often faster because they can change habits quickly.

The mental model is simple: one clear definition, one clear workflow, and configuration that matches reality. Most confusion around modules and organization comes from mixing those layers — mixing definitions from different eras, or configs copied from projects with different needs. Keep them separate and the rest of this guide follows naturally.

Why Bother with Modules and organization?

"Why bother" is a fair question for any tooling topic. Modules and organization earns its keep in three concrete ways:

  • It reduces build and integration surprises, the kind that derail an otherwise calm sprint.
  • It cuts repetitive work out of the daily loop, which is where most of the hidden cost of tooling lives.
  • It turns tribal knowledge into something a newcomer can read, run, and verify.

Notice what is missing from that list: fashion. None of these benefits expire with a release cycle, which is why modules and organization keeps showing up in every serious F# conversation. Everything below assumes the current 2026 toolchain, but the reasoning generalizes to older versions too.

Advanced Tips

Once the fundamentals are second nature, these are the practices that separate good setups from great ones:

  • Record decisions next to the code they affect, in the same pull request that introduces them.
  • Version your conventions: what worked for a two-person team needs revision at ten, and that is normal.
  • Automate the audit: a scheduled check that compares expected state to real state catches drift within hours instead of months.
  • Tip: One module per domain concept, nested when it aids reading
