What Is Assembly trimming?

Assembly trimming, in one sentence: removing unused IL from published output. In practice that means the details are worth getting right once, because everything downstream inherits the decision. Once the defaults are deliberate, the rest of the workflow tends to fall into place.

The mental model is simple: one clear definition, one clear workflow, and configuration that matches reality. Most confusion around assembly trimming comes from mixing those layers — mixing definitions from different eras, or configs copied from projects with different needs. Keep them separate and the rest of this guide follows naturally.

Why Bother with Assembly trimming?

"Why bother" is a fair question for any tooling topic. Assembly trimming earns its keep in three concrete ways:

  • It makes onboarding dramatically easier: new developers find rails instead of folklore.
  • It keeps dependencies and toolchains healthy, which quietly prevents future emergencies.
  • It shortens feedback loops, so problems surface while the code is still in your head.

Notice what is missing from that list: fashion. None of these benefits expire with a release cycle, which is why assembly trimming keeps showing up in every serious .NET conversation. It is one of the few practices that pays for itself within the first week of consistent use.

Common Assembly trimming Mistakes

The same failure patterns show up again and again. Here are the four we meet most often, including the classic: Trimming reflection paths that configuration or DI discovers at runtime.

  • Assuming the default configuration matches your project's real requirements.
  • Optimizing before measuring, then optimizing again after the metrics disagree.
  • Treating assembly trimming as a one-time setup instead of a living part of the workflow.
  • Copying configuration from an old project without understanding what it does today.

Every one of these fixes is cheap when caught early and expensive when caught late. A quarterly review of assembly trimming — twenty minutes, honestly — catches most of them before they metastasize.

Assembly trimming vs Dotnet local tools

They overlap enough that the question is usually about trade-offs rather than right and wrong. Assembly trimming optimizes for container images and single-file apps get much smaller; Dotnet local tools pushes harder on the opposite axis. List your constraints honestly — team experience, platform targets, and maintenance budget — and the answer usually picks itself.

Start from the problem, not the tool. If your pain is container images and single-file apps get much smaller, Assembly trimming is the more direct route; if your team already lives in the other ecosystem, Dotnet local tools minimizes ramp-up cost. Whatever you choose, write down why, because the next person will ask.

Quick Example

Here is a compact, real-world command prompt example you can adapt directly. It shows the configuration style we recommend for assembly trimming: explicit, commented where it matters, and free of magic.

dotnet publish -c Release -r linux-x64 --self-contained \
  -p:PublishSingleFile=true -p:PublishTrimmed=true

Copy it, run it once, then trim it to what your project actually needs. The worst version of any snippet is the one nobody on the team fully understands.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need Assembly trimming for small projects?

More than you would expect. Small projects benefit disproportionately because there is no dedicated ops person to catch drift — the setup protects itself.

Can Assembly trimming be automated?

Yes, and it should be. Start by scripting the steps in this guide, then wire them into CI so the automation runs without anyone remembering to.

What are the most common mistakes with Assembly trimming?

The big three: treating it as a one-time setup, letting local configuration drift from CI, and skipping documentation for the decisions you make. All three are covered above with concrete fixes.

Is Assembly trimming still relevant in 2026?
