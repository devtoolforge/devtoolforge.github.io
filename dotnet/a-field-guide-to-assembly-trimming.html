What Is Assembly trimming?

Assembly trimming, in one sentence: removing unused IL from published output. In practice that means the details are worth getting right once, because everything downstream inherits the decision. Once the defaults are deliberate, the rest of the workflow tends to fall into place.

The mental model is simple: one clear definition, one clear workflow, and configuration that matches reality. Most confusion around assembly trimming comes from mixing those layers — mixing definitions from different eras, or configs copied from projects with different needs. Keep them separate and the rest of this guide follows naturally.

The Mechanics of Assembly trimming

Publish with -p:PublishTrimmed=true and test the trimmed app thoroughly. That is the core of it, and the steps below turn the idea into muscle memory:

Common Assembly trimming Mistakes

The same failure patterns show up again and again. Here are the four we meet most often, including the classic: Trimming reflection paths that configuration or DI discovers at runtime.

  • Treating assembly trimming as a one-time setup instead of a living part of the workflow.
  • Assuming the default configuration matches your project's real requirements.
  • Shipping changes without a rollback plan and improvising during the incident.
  • Letting local settings drift from CI until nobody knows which one is right.

Every one of these fixes is cheap when caught early and expensive when caught late. A quarterly review of assembly trimming — twenty minutes, honestly — catches most of them before they metastasize.

Performance Considerations

Remove work before making work faster. The cheapest optimization for assembly trimming is usually doing less: fewer allocations, fewer passes, fewer round trips. Profile to find work that can be skipped entirely under normal conditions. Only then reach for caching, and only with an explicit invalidation plan.

Change one thing at a time. Stacked optimizations hide which change actually helped and which added complexity for nothing. Re-run the same measurement after each adjustment to assembly trimming and keep a short log of results. When two changes interact, test them separately before combining, or you will ship mystery performance.

Troubleshooting Assembly trimming

When things break, symptoms cluster into a handful of patterns. Match yours here and jump straight to the fix:

  • It worked yesterday and nothing was changed — check what the OS, SDK, or package manager updated overnight before blaming the code
  • It works on one machine and fails on another — diff the versions and configuration first — environment drift explains most of these bugs
  • Performance collapsed after an upgrade — bisect the upgrade by rolling back one component at a time and re-measuring

If none of these match, capture a full log before changing anything. For assembly trimming that log almost always names the failing layer, and the fix becomes obvious instead of guessed.

A Practical Workflow

A repeatable workflow turns assembly trimming from a personality trait into a team capability:

  1. Add a check to CI so regressions in assembly trimming fail loudly instead of silently.
  2. Pick the smallest meaningful change you can ship and measure — momentum matters more than scope.
  3. Write the chosen setup down in the repository so the decision survives the next refactor.
  4. Document the rollback path before you need it, not during an incident.
  5. Automate the step so it runs without anyone remembering the manual command.

This loop — audit, change, automate, verify — is deliberately generic. It fits .NET at any scale and survives every tooling generation we have seen.
